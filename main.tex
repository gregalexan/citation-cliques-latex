\begin{abstract}
Do authors who publish mainly in lower-impact journals cite within tighter, more mutual circles than comparable authors who publish mainly in higher-impact journals?  We compare \MatchedPairCount\ pairs of authors matched within broad subject and on recent citation productivity, using a fixed Crossref snapshot for 2020--2024.  On average, lower-impact-group authors directed more of their citations to previous collaborators, to authors who cited them back, and to a few recipients, and their citation neighbors were more often connected to each other.  They were also more likely to belong to small groups of authors who cite one another heavily (reciprocal citation cliques): \CliqueCaseMembershipPercent\% compared with \CliqueControlMembershipPercent\%.  The direction held across clique definitions and subjects, although its size varied by field.  Most authors in both groups had no such ties, and both groups cited mostly within their own group.  These patterns are consistent with several explanations, including specialized or regional research communities, stable collaborations, and strategic citation exchange; structural data alone cannot separate them.  Cohesion indicators are therefore best read as context for evaluation and as a way to prioritize qualitative review, not as evidence of misconduct.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} citation networks; reciprocal citation; citation cohesion; journal impact; collaboration; research evaluation

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Citation indicators are widely used to summarize scholarly influence, allocate resources, and support evaluation.  Their convenience also creates incentives to optimize what is measured, which can weaken the link between an indicator and the quality it is meant to represent \parencite{Goo84,Hicks2015,Biagioli2019}.  Unusual citation practices, such as excessive self-citation, coercive citation, and organized citation exchange, have therefore become objects of measurement in their own right \parencite{Wilhite2012,VanNoorden2019,Biagioli2020}.

Much of this attention concerns \emph{cohesion}: the extent to which an author's citations stay within a small, interconnected set of people.  Cohesion is ambiguous.  A tightly knit citation neighborhood can mean that an author works in a narrow specialty in which only a few groups study the same problem, that the author belongs to a stable team whose members build on each other's work, or that the author participates in a regional or language community with its own journals.  It can also reflect deliberate exchange of citations.  Theories of citing have long disagreed about how far citations record intellectual debt and how far they serve social and persuasive purposes \parencite{Merton1988,Gilbert1977,Bornmann2008,Tahamtan2019}, and the same structure can arise from either.  Measuring cohesion is therefore useful only if the measurement is interpreted against these competing explanations.

This study asks whether cohesion differs between authors who publish mainly in lower-impact and mainly in higher-impact journals.  The comparison is informative because journals are stratified: the same article receives more citations when it appears in a higher-impact journal \parencite{Lariviere2010}, and lower-impact venues may more often serve smaller specialties or national and regional communities.  If lower-impact venues host more closed citation communities, author-level cohesion should be higher there even among authors with similar citation records.  Most existing evidence on unusual citation structure is either journal-level \parencite{Kojaku2021,heneberg2016} or starts from groups already selected for unusual behavior.  Author-level concentration indicators show when a small set of authors accounts for much of an author's citation impact \parencite{Evdaimon2024}, but less is known about how cohesion is distributed across journal-impact groups once authors are compared within subject and at similar productivity.

We use a matched design.  Each author who publishes mostly in lower-impact journals of a subject (a \emph{Case}) is paired with an author who publishes mostly in higher-impact journals of the same subject (a \emph{Control}) and has a similar recent $h$-index.  The labels are compact names for these two groups and carry no judgment of scientific merit.  We ask three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Do matched Cases and Controls differ in how cohesive and concentrated their citation links are, and in how often they belong to reciprocal citation cliques?
  \item Are author profiles that are unusual relative to Controls more common among Cases?
  \item Do the two groups cite each other, or does each group cite mainly within itself?
\end{enumerate}

We find that Cases are more cohesive on every primary measure and are \CliqueMembershipRatio\ times as likely as their Controls to belong to reciprocal citation cliques, in every subject and under every clique definition we tried.  At the same time, most authors in both groups have no such ties, both groups cite mainly within their own group, and Cases reference their own journal less often than Controls.  We interpret these results as describing differences in the structure of research communities rather than in the behavior of individuals, and we discuss which explanations the data can and cannot distinguish.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Why authors cite.}  The normative view holds that citations acknowledge intellectual debts and therefore track use and influence \parencite{Merton1988}.  The social-constructivist view treats citations as rhetorical tools that persuade readers and position a paper within a community \parencite{Gilbert1977}.  Reviews of citing motivations find evidence for both and conclude that papers are cited for scientific and non-scientific reasons \parencite{Bornmann2008,Tahamtan2019}.  In an economy of attention, a citation is also a payment that the cited author can return, which gives reciprocal exchange a rationale \parencite{Franck1999}.  For this study the key implication is that a cohesive citation neighborhood is not, by itself, informative about motive: it can arise because a small community genuinely builds on its own work, or because its members cite each other for social reasons.

\paragraph{Collaboration and self-citation.}  Authors cite their collaborators more often than other authors, and collaboration networks shape who cites whom \parencite{Wallace2012,Martin2013}.  Citations to coauthors can be seen as an extended form of self-citation \parencite{Ioannidis2015}.  Self-citation itself is common, differs across fields, and is associated with more citations from others \parencite{Snyder1998,Aksnes2003,Fowler2007,Ioannidis2019}; it also responds to evaluation incentives \parencite{Seeber2019,Baccini2019}.  Cohesion measures that count citations to prior coauthors or to oneself therefore partly measure collaboration structure.  That structure differs across fields: collaboration networks are highly clustered, and team size has grown in nearly all fields \parencite{Newman2001,Moody2004,Wuchty2007}.

\paragraph{Reciprocity, citation rings, and cartels.}  At the journal level, groups of journals that cite each other far more than expected have been documented and, in some cases, sanctioned \parencite{heneberg2016,Kojaku2021}.  Network methods have been proposed to locate such groups \parencite{Fister2016}, and evaluation regimes based on citation counts have been shown to change the citation behavior of researchers \parencite{Baccini2019}.  Cartel-like mutual citation can also arise from institutional practice without coordination, as when each class of journals prefers to cite its own members \parencite{Perez2019}.  At the author level, Evdaimon et al.\ propose indicators of how concentrated an author's citing base is \parencite{Evdaimon2024}.  These studies establish that relational structure carries information, but they either work at the venue level or begin from known cases.  Reciprocity itself is ambiguous: mutual citation is also the normal state of an active research front in which a few groups respond to each other.

\paragraph{Specialization, communities, and journal stratification.}  Citation networks have been studied as the structure of research fronts since \textcite{Price1965}, and the idea that scientists form informal communities, or invisible colleges, has a long history, although Crane's survey found these communities more open than the term suggests \parencite{Crane1969}.  Citation links encode topical communities well \parencite{Boyack2010}, and papers with overlapping bibliographies are more likely to cite each other \parencite{Ciotti2016}, so dense citation is expected wherever topics are narrow.  Journals are stratified as well: an identical article is cited more when it appears in a higher-impact journal \parencite{Lariviere2010}.  Citation preferences for authors of the same country are documented \parencite{Qiu2025,Baccini2023}, which suggests that journals serving national or language communities may also show more internal citation.  Citation levels and practices differ strongly across fields, which is why indicators are normalized by field \parencite{Waltman2016}.

\paragraph{Gap addressed.}  Together, this work suggests two opposite expectations.  If lower-impact journals mainly host specialized or regional communities, their authors should show more cohesive citation neighborhoods for structural reasons.  If they also host more strategic exchange, the same pattern would appear.  Neither expectation has been tested with a matched author-level comparison across journal-impact groups.  We provide that comparison, report how the difference varies across fields and with collaboration, and use the literature above to interpret what the differences can and cannot mean.

\subsection{Data and journal groups}
\label{sec:data}

The source is a fixed Crossref snapshot processed with \textit{alexandria3k} \parencite{Spinellis2023}, covering works and references dated 2020--2024.  Authors are identified by ORCID, a persistent researcher identifier.  Journals are assigned to five broad subjects: Health Sciences, Life Sciences, Physical Sciences, Social Sciences and Humanities, and Multidisciplinary.

Journal impact is measured by the Eigenfactor score, which rates a journal by the citations it receives from other journals, weighting each citation by the influence of the citing journal and excluding journal self-citations \parencite{West2008}.  We use it rather than a raw citation average because it is less sensitive to a journal citing itself, which matters in a study about closed citation patterns.  The score measures a journal's total influence, so it also grows with the number of articles the journal publishes; the lower-impact group is therefore expected to include many small journals.  Within each subject, journals at or below the 25th percentile of scores form the lower-impact group and journals at or above the 75th percentile form the higher-impact group; percentiles are weighted by the number of articles.  The two cutoffs leave the middle half of output out, so the groups are clearly separated, and computing them within subject respects the large differences in citation levels between fields \parencite{Waltman2016}.

\subsection{Authors and matching}
\label{sec:matching}

The unit of analysis is an \emph{author--subject}: one researcher considered within one subject.  An author is a Case in a subject when at least 70\% of their eligible works there appear in lower-impact journals, and a Control when at least 70\% appear in higher-impact journals; authors also need at least three works in the subject.  The 70\% rule assigns authors whose output is mostly in one group while allowing some publication elsewhere, which is typical for active researchers.

Authors who publish more and are cited more have more citation links, and therefore more chances to show any network pattern.  To compare like with like, each Case is paired with a Control from the same subject whose $h_5$ is similar.  Here $h_5$ is the $h$-index computed from the author's works in that subject published in 2020--2024, the same window in which citation behavior is measured \parencite{Hirsch2005}.  Pairs could differ by at most three points, and the closest available pairs were chosen first in a fixed order.  In practice matches were much closer than this limit: \ExactHFivePairPercent\% of pairs have identical $h_5$, and no pair differs by more than \MaximumAbsoluteHFiveDifference\ (Table~\ref{tab:matching-balance}).  A sensitivity check repeats the main comparisons on exact matches only.

\subsection{Citation links and measures}
\label{sec:measures}

We treat authors as nodes and citations as directed links from the citing to the cited author.  Each work-to-work citation is split equally among all author combinations of the two works, so a citation between two large teams does not count more than one between two single authors.  Links are built separately within each subject, and a citation from $i$ to $j$ counts as a citation to a prior coauthor only if the two had published together before the citation year.

Table~\ref{tab:measures} lists the eight measures.  Four primary measures capture cohesion and concentration: citations to prior coauthors, reciprocity, local clustering, and concentration of outgoing citations.  Four secondary measures add context: self-citation, citation of one's own journal, the balance between citations sent and received, and the largest one-year rise in citations to one recipient.  Measures based on links between peers use only links among matched authors, so they describe how the matched authors cite each other rather than the full citation network.  The formulas and the rules for zeros and missing values are given in Appendix~\ref{app:measures}.

\subsection{Reciprocal citation cliques}
\label{sec:cliques}

The measures above describe each author separately.  To describe groups, we look for \emph{reciprocal citation cliques}: sets of matched authors in the same subject who all cite one another and whose citations largely flow in both directions.  A candidate group is a largest possible set of at least four authors in which every two members are linked by a citation in at least one direction.  A candidate is a reciprocal clique when at least 75\% of all possible directed links among members are present and at least half of the citation weight exchanged between members is returned.  Figure~\ref{fig:clique-concept} illustrates the two conditions.

These thresholds express the idea of a closed, mutual group in simple terms.  Three mutually citing authors are common in any active field and can be a single team, so four is the smallest size we treat as a group.  The 75\% density threshold allows a few missing links in an otherwise complete group, and the 50\% reciprocity threshold requires exchange to be at least as much two-way as one-way.  Because any threshold is a choice, we repeated the analysis for minimum sizes of three, four, and five and for reciprocity thresholds of 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75.

A reciprocal clique is a structural pattern, not a judgment.  A specialized research front, a long-standing collaboration, or a group exchanging citations by agreement can all produce one.  To see how much of the pattern reflects collaboration, we repeated the analysis after removing all citation links between authors who had published together before the citation year.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Matched comparisons.}  For each pair and measure, we subtract the Control value from the Case value.  Because the measures are bounded, skewed, and often zero, we use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which compares the ranks of the paired differences without assuming a normal distribution \parencite{Wilcoxon1945}.  We report the mean paired difference with a bootstrap 95\% confidence interval \parencite{Efron1993} and a rank effect size from $-1$ to 1 that shows how consistently Cases rank above their Controls.  $P$-values are adjusted for the four tests in each family \parencite{Benjamini1995}.  Each comparison uses the pairs in which both authors have a defined value.  Many authors have no links to other matched authors, so medians of peer-based measures are often zero; the mean difference and the rank effect then show the direction and size of the shift.  For clique membership we compare, within pairs, how often only the Case or only the Control belongs to a clique, using an exact test.

\paragraph{Screening for unusual profiles.}  The second question asks whether profiles that are unusual compared with Controls are more common among Cases.  We combine five author-level measures (self-citation, prior-coauthor citation, reciprocity, clustering, and outgoing concentration) and score each author--subject with an Isolation Forest, a method that gives high scores to profiles that are easy to separate from the rest \parencite{Liu2008}.  The model and its thresholds are fitted to Controls only, within each subject.  A profile is flagged when its score is above the 99th percentile of Control scores and at least two cohesion measures are also above their Control 99th percentiles.  Using percentiles of the observed Control distribution avoids assuming that bibliometric measures are normally distributed.  The screen does not predict misconduct; it orders profiles for possible qualitative review.  We varied the threshold, the random seed, and the set of inputs (Appendix~\ref{app:screen}).

\paragraph{Group mixing.}  The third question asks where each group's citations go.  We sum the citation weight among matched authors from Cases and Controls to Cases and Controls and compare the share that stays within the citing group with the share expected if citations were spread in proportion to the totals \parencite{Newman2002}.  A test that swaps the two labels within matched pairs checks whether the observed share could arise from the pairing alone.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Cases have more cohesive and concentrated citation neighborhoods}
\label{sec:paired-results}

On all four primary measures, Cases scored higher on average than their matched Controls (Figure~\ref{fig:paired-effects}, Table~\ref{tab:paired-primary}).  The clearest difference was in outgoing concentration: the median Case concentrated its citations on fewer recipients than the median Control (HHI \PrimaryOutgoingHhiCaseMedian\ versus \PrimaryOutgoingHhiControlMedian), and the rank effect was large (\PrimaryOutgoingHhiRankBiserial).  Reciprocity and local clustering were also higher for Cases (rank effects \PrimaryReciprocityRankBiserial\ and \PrimaryLocalClusteringRankBiserial).  For these two measures both group medians were zero because most authors in both groups had no reciprocated or clustered links to other matched authors; the differences come from the minority of authors who have such links.

The prior-coauthor measure shows the same pattern in a different form.  The median Case cited prior coauthors less than the median Control (\PrimaryCoauthorCitationRateCaseMedian\ versus \PrimaryCoauthorCitationRateControlMedian), yet the mean difference and rank effect favored Cases (rank effect \PrimaryCoauthorCitationRateRankBiserial).  Most Cases rarely cite prior collaborators, but those who do direct a large share of their citations to them.  Self-citation followed a similar pattern.

Two secondary results point away from a simple story of insularity.  Cases referenced articles in their own journal less often than Controls (median \SecondaryJournalEndogamyCaseMedian\ versus \SecondaryJournalEndogamyControlMedian), and the balance between citations sent and received among matched peers barely differed.  Cases had higher surge shares, meaning that a larger part of their citations came from a rise in citing one recipient in one year (Table~\ref{tab:paired-secondary}).

These results were robust.  Restricting the comparison to the exact-$h_5$ pairs, and counting collaborations formed in the citation year as prior, left the direction of all primary results unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:exact-h5}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Authors who publish mainly in lower-impact journals have, on average, more cohesive and concentrated citation neighborhoods than matched authors who publish mainly in higher-impact journals, and they are \CliqueMembershipRatio\ times as likely to belong to a reciprocal citation clique.  The difference appears in every subject and under every clique definition we examined.  Three features of the results shape how this finding should be read: most authors in both groups have no cohesive ties at all, both groups cite mainly within themselves, and Cases are less, not more, likely to cite their own journal.

\paragraph{What cohesion can mean.}  Figure~\ref{fig:explanations} summarizes four explanations for cohesive structure and what our data can say about each.  The normative and social-constructivist accounts of citing predict the same structure for different reasons \parencite{Merton1988,Gilbert1977,Bornmann2008}.  A small specialty in which a few groups respond to each other produces reciprocity, clustering, and concentration because there are few relevant authors to cite.  If lower-impact journals more often serve such specialties, or national and language communities, their authors would show more cohesion even if every citation were intellectually motivated; topical overlap alone raises the chance of citation \parencite{Ciotti2016}, and preferences for one's own country and one's own class of journals are documented \parencite{Qiu2025,Perez2019}.  The lower same-journal referencing of Cases fits this reading.  Because Eigenfactor scores grow with the number of articles a journal publishes, the lower-impact group is expected to include many smaller journals; one likely reason for the difference is that their authors have fewer articles in their own journal to cite and must cite across venues, while still citing a narrow set of people.  Strategic exchange would produce the same author-level structure.  The data identify where cohesive structure is concentrated; they do not identify which mechanism produced it.

\paragraph{Collaboration and teams.}  Authors cite their collaborators, and collaboration networks shape citation flows \parencite{Wallace2012}.  The prior-coauthor results show that this channel is uneven among Cases: most cite prior collaborators rarely, but a minority direct much of their citation weight to them, consistent with small, stable teams whose members build on one another.  The collaboration-excluded clique analysis shows how far reciprocal cliques depend on such ties.

% links, and interpret: persistence means cliques are not only research teams;
% a large reduction means collaboration explains much of the pattern.
Because team size and collaboration intensity differ by field and over time \parencite{Wuchty2007,Newman2001}, part of the field variation we observe probably reflects different collaboration cultures rather than different citation norms.

\paragraph{Field differences.}  The clique gap points in the same direction in every subject but varies widely in size, and cliques are rare in some subjects for both groups.  This mirrors the well-known variation of citation density and practice across fields \parencite{Waltman2016}.  It also implies that a single threshold for ``unusual'' cohesion would mostly flag fields with dense citation practices.  Any practical use of these measures should therefore compare authors within field, as our matched design does.

\paragraph{Citing within one's own group.}  The strong within-group citation preference is not specific to Cases: Controls cite other Controls about as much as Cases cite other Cases.  Part of this is expected by construction, because the groups are defined by sets of journals and authors tend to cite the communities they publish in.  The result is better read as evidence that lower- and higher-impact journals host largely separate citation communities, in line with the view of citation networks as maps of research communities \parencite{Price1965,Boyack2010}, than as evidence that one group closes itself off.  The law-review case shows that such class-based preferences can arise from institutional practice alone \parencite{Perez2019}.

\paragraph{Relation to prior detection work.}  Journal-level studies identify groups of journals with anomalous mutual citation \parencite{Kojaku2021,heneberg2016,Fister2016}.  Our results show that a related pattern exists among authors and that it is more common among authors of lower-impact journals, but the populations, measures, and comparisons differ, so the estimates are not interchangeable.  The concentration measure used here complements the citing-base concentration of \textcite{Evdaimon2024}: theirs asks how few authors account for an author's received citations, ours how few recipients account for the citations an author sends.  Evidence that evaluation incentives can change citation behavior \parencite{Baccini2019,Seeber2019} makes it plausible that some cohesive groups reflect strategic exchange, but our design cannot say which.

\paragraph{Implications for scientometric practice.}  First, cohesion is a property of communities as much as of individuals.  An author's reciprocity or clustering should be compared with that of similar authors in the same field and venue group before it is treated as unusual.  Second, cohesion indicators are most useful as context, for instance when interpreting a high citation count that comes mostly from a small, mutually citing group, in line with the principle that quantitative evaluation should support rather than replace expert judgment \parencite{Hicks2015,Wilsdon2015}.  Field-specific reference distributions have already been proposed for separating influence from excess self-citation \parencite{Szomszor2020}, and cohesion could be treated in the same way.  Third, screens such as ours can help prioritize which groups deserve qualitative review, but even a stable, conservative screen flags profiles whose structure has legitimate explanations.  Fourth, journal-impact group should not be used as a proxy for individual behavior: most authors in lower-impact journals showed no cohesive ties at all.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Conceptual limits.}  A reciprocal clique is a pattern of links, not a description of intent.  Specialized research fronts, long-term collaborations, and agreed exchange all produce it, and citation metadata cannot reveal the content or function of a citation \parencite{Liu2024}.  Topical proximity is not measured: the five broad subjects cannot tell whether two authors work on the same narrow problem, which is the most important alternative explanation for cohesion.  Journal-impact group bundles several characteristics, including journal size, audience, language, region, and editorial practice, so differences between groups cannot be attributed to impact as such.  Peer-based measures and cliques use only links among matched authors and describe how these authors cite each other, not their position in the full citation network.  Outgoing concentration and surge share are mechanically higher for authors who cite fewer identified recipients, so differences between journal groups in reference-list length or in the ORCID coverage of cited authors could contribute to these two results.  We do not use authors' country or language, so regional and language communities are discussed but not examined.  Finally, the design is associational: matching on subject and $h_5$ does not balance career stage, institution, country, team size, or topic.

\paragraph{Data and measurement.}  The results apply to a fixed 2020--2024 Crossref snapshot and to authors with ORCID identifiers who meet the inclusion rules; ORCID coverage may differ across fields and countries.  Journals were assigned to subjects by a language model without a human validation sample (Appendix~\ref{app:venue-scores}).  The five-year window limits the dating of earlier collaborations, and we do not model how the gap changes over time.  The screen is an operational rule without ground-truth labels; sensitivity analyses show it is stable but cannot show that it is accurate.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Citation cohesion is more common among authors who publish mainly in lower-impact journals than among comparable authors in higher-impact journals, and membership in reciprocal citation cliques is \CliqueMembershipRatio\ times as frequent.  The pattern holds across subjects and definitions but varies in size by field, and it coexists with the fact that most authors in both groups show no such ties.  The most likely readings involve the community structure of lower-impact venues as well as, possibly, strategic exchange; structural data alone cannot separate them.  For research evaluation, cohesion indicators are best used within field, as context, and as a starting point for qualitative review.  Finer topic data, collaboration-aware indicators, and qualitative study of flagged groups are natural next steps.

\section{Venue Scores and Journal Groups}
\label{app:venue-scores}

The appendices document the construction rules, formulas, audits, and sensitivity analyses that support the main text.  All numbers are generated by the same analysis run as the main text, and the replication package \parencite{dataset_zenodo} contains the code that produces every table.

\paragraph{Journal scores.}  For subject $s$, let $Z^{(s)}_{jk}$ count citations from citing journal $j$ to cited journal $k$.  The diagonal is removed, and each non-dangling citing-journal column of $H_s$ is
\begin{equation}
  (H_s)_{kj}=\frac{Z^{(s)}_{jk}}{\sum_{\ell}Z^{(s)}_{j\ell}}.
\end{equation}
Let $a_s$ be the normalized journal article-count vector and $d_s$ indicate dangling columns.  With damping $\alpha=0.85$, the subject-specific influence vector is obtained by
\begin{equation}
  \pi_s=\alpha H_s\pi_s+
  \left[\alpha d_s^\mathsf{T}\pi_s+(1-\alpha)\right]a_s.
\end{equation}
The received-influence vector $H_s\pi_s$ is normalized to sum to 100 within subject \parencite{West2008}.  Citation mass flows from citing to cited journals, journal self-citations are excluded, and dangling and restart mass follow article counts.  The scores are fixed inputs taken from the snapshot; they are not re-estimated in this study.

\paragraph{Group cutoffs.}  After scores are attached to eligible 2020--2024 work rows, the 25th and 75th percentile cutoffs are calculated across positive-score work rows within each subject.  A journal therefore contributes once per eligible work, so the cutoffs are publication weighted rather than percentiles over distinct journals.  Journals at or below the lower cutoff form the lower-impact group, and journals at or above the upper cutoff form the higher-impact group.

\paragraph{Journal subject classification.}  Journals are assigned to Health Sciences, Life Sciences, Physical Sciences, Social Sciences and Humanities, or Multidisciplinary, the four subject areas of the Scopus All Science Journal Classification (ASJC) plus a multidisciplinary class; the replication files store them as codes 1 to 5 in alphabetical order.  The mapping was produced with OpenAI \texttt{gpt-4.1} (snapshot \path{gpt-4.1-2025-04-14}) at zero temperature from journal names and representative work titles and abstracts.  Prompts and cached outputs are part of the replication materials.  The mapping has no independent human-validation sample and no open-weight baseline, so classification error is a limitation rather than a measured source of uncertainty \parencite{LLMGuidelines2024}.

\section{Cohort and Matching}
\label{app:matching}

\paragraph{Eligibility.}  Cases and Controls require a non-empty ORCID, at least three eligible works in the subject, and at least 70\% of those works in the relevant journal group.  The cohort uses every ORCID in the snapshot that meets these rules.  The unit is an author--subject $(\mathrm{ORCID},s)$, and a uniqueness check prevents more than one group assignment for the same key.  The matching pool additionally requires $h_5\geq1$, where $h_5$ is the $h$-index computed from the author's subject-$s$ works published 2020--2024 and citations in the snapshot \parencite{Hirsch2005}.

\paragraph{Matching rule.}  Candidate pairs must belong to the same subject and satisfy $|h_{5,\mathrm{Case}}-h_{5,\mathrm{Control}}|\leq3$.  Greedy selection proceeds without replacement within subject in the fixed order subject, ascending absolute $h_5$ difference, Case ORCID, and Control ORCID.  The matching table supplies the Case ORCID, Control ORCID, subject, and both $h_5$ values.  Author features are linked back to this table on $(\mathrm{case\_orcid},s)$ and $(\mathrm{control\_orcid},s)$; joins on ORCID alone are not used.

\paragraph{Audit.}  An independent audit repeats the deterministic match from the generated cohort profiles and compares it with the stored pairs.  It requires identical order and membership, no pair beyond the three-point limit, and no reused author--subject key.  The audit found a maximum observed absolute $h_5$ difference of \MaximumAbsoluteHFiveDifference\ and \HFiveCaliperViolationCount\ pairs beyond the caliper.

\section{Citation Network Construction}
\label{app:construction}

\paragraph{Reference resolution and fractional weights.}  A citing-work/cited-work DOI pair is deduplicated before either work is expanded to author rows.  Let $n_c$ and $n_a$ be the complete counts of author rows on the citing and cited work, including authors without ORCIDs.  A resolved work citation contributes
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{n_c n_a}
\end{equation}
to each identified citing-author/identified cited-author combination.  Rows with an unidentified endpoint produce no author edge, and missing identifiers cannot become a shared pseudo-author.  Several matched authors on one citing work do not multiply the underlying work reference.

\paragraph{Edge key and subject.}  Every edge is keyed by subject, citing ORCID, cited ORCID, and citation year, and duplicate rows are aggregated so that this key is unique.  Only citing works assigned to the matched author's subject contribute to that author--subject's measures.

\paragraph{Prior collaboration.}  Let $c_{ij}$ be the first year in which authors $i$ and $j$ published together.  A citation from $i$ to $j$ in year $t$ is a prior-coauthor citation only if $c_{ij}<t$.  A sensitivity analysis uses $c_{ij}\leq t$ (Appendix~\ref{app:paired}).

\paragraph{Three views of the links.}  Each measure is computed on one of three populations:
\begin{description}
  \item[Outgoing links of each author.] Citations from matched author--subject $i$ to any identified author.  Used for self-citation, prior-coauthor citation, outgoing concentration, and surge share.
  \item[Links among matched authors.] For each subject $s$, only matched authors and the links between them.  Used for reciprocity, local clustering, citation balance, group mixing, and cliques.
  \item[Deduplicated work references.] Built before author expansion.  Used for same-journal referencing, which does not require the cited author to be identified.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Notation.}  Let $x_{ijt}$ be the fractional citation weight from author $i$ to author $j$ in year $t$; missing dyad--years are zero, and $w_{ij}=\sum_{t=2020}^{2024}x_{ijt}$.  If $V_s$ is the set of matched authors in subject $s$, the matched-author graph is the directed, weighted, loopless graph
\begin{equation}
  G_s=(V_s,E_s,w),\qquad
  E_s=\{(i,j):i,j\in V_s,\ i\ne j,\ w_{ij}>0\}.
\end{equation}
Its simple undirected projection is $U_s=(V_s,\widetilde E_s)$ with $\{i,j\}\in\widetilde E_s$ if and only if $i\ne j$ and $w_{ij}+w_{ji}>0$.  Direction and weight are discarded only in $U_s$, which is used for local clustering.  Self-edges remain in the outgoing view but do not enter $G_s$.

\paragraph{Zeros and missing values.}  Unavailable values remain missing.  A zero is used only when the denominator and graph population are observed but the relevant event is absent.  A defined rate with no qualifying citations is zero; reciprocity is zero when internal outgoing weight exists but none is returned; clustering is zero for an observed matched-graph node with fewer than two neighbors; and same-journal referencing is zero when resolved references exist but none stays within the citing journal.  Outgoing HHI is missing when there is no identified non-self outgoing weight, balance is missing when total internal weight is zero, same-journal referencing is missing when no resolved outgoing reference exists, and surge share is missing when total identified outgoing weight is zero.  These rules imply that surge share lies in $[0,1]$.  Each comparison drops pairs with a missing value for that measure only.

\paragraph{Surge share and burst detection.}  The surge share describes the largest one-year rise in citation weight toward one recipient.  It is a simple descriptive increment and is not a state-transition burst-detection model.

\section{Paired Inference Details and Sensitivity}
\label{app:paired}

For each pair and measure, the Control value is subtracted from the Case value.  The two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test is used because the measures are bounded, skewed, and tied at zero \parencite{Wilcoxon1945}.  The rank-biserial effect is
\begin{equation}
  r_{\mathrm{rb}}=\frac{W^+-W^-}{W^++W^-},
\end{equation}
where $W^+$ and $W^-$ sum the ranks of positive and negative differences after zero differences are removed.  Intervals for median and mean differences come from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of pair identifiers \parencite{Efron1993}.  Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment is applied within the primary and within the secondary family, not across them \parencite{Benjamini1995}.  A sign-flip randomization test reverses the sign of each pair difference in 10,000 runs; its $p$-values are reported in \texttt{paired\_primary.csv} and \texttt{paired\_secondary.csv} of the replication package.  The same-year collaboration check changes only $c_{ij}<t$ to $c_{ij}\leq t$.

\section{Reciprocal-Clique Specification and Sensitivity}
\label{app:cliques}

A candidate group $C$ is a maximal set of at least $k$ matched authors of one subject in which every pair is connected in $U_s$.  For a group of size $k$ with $m_C$ positive directed dyads, directed density is
\begin{equation}
  d_C=\frac{m_C}{k(k-1)},
\end{equation}
and weighted reciprocity is
\begin{equation}
  r_C=\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset C}\min(w_{ij},w_{ji})}
           {\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset C}\max(w_{ij},w_{ji})}.
\end{equation}
The primary rule requires $k\geq4$, $d_C\geq0.75$, and $r_C\geq0.50$.  Overlapping qualifying groups are converted to one binary membership value per author--subject.  The exact two-sided matched test conditions on the number of discordant pairs and gives either member of each discordant pair equal probability of being the clique member.  The collaboration check repeats the full analysis after removing every annual edge with $c_{ij}<t$.

\section{Anomaly-Screen Specification and Sensitivity}
\label{app:screen}

\paragraph{Population.}  An author--subject is eligible when it has at least one identified non-self outgoing citation, and screenable when all five inputs (self-citation, prior-coauthor citation, reciprocity, local clustering, outgoing HHI) are defined.  Missing inputs are not set to zero; such rows cannot be flagged and are reported separately.  Same-journal referencing is excluded because it uses work references, citation balance because it requires internal incident weight, and surge share because it is temporal.

\paragraph{Scaling and detector.}  Within each subject, every input is centered at the Control median and divided by the Control interquartile range; a zero or nonfinite Control interquartile range is replaced by 1 only to avoid division by zero.  An Isolation Forest \parencite{Liu2008} is fitted to Controls with seed~42.  The anomaly score is the negative of the library's \texttt{score\_samples} output, and the library's \texttt{contamination=auto} cutoff is not used.  No feature weights are applied.

\paragraph{Flag.}  A row passes the first rule when its score exceeds the 99th percentile of Control scores in the same subject, and the second rule when at least two of the four cohesion inputs exceed their Control 99th percentiles.  The final flag requires both.  Because percentile thresholds are empirical, no normality assumption is made.  Because the cohesion inputs also enter the detector, the two rules overlap by construction (Table~\ref{tab:anomaly-overlap}).  \AnomalyOverlapFindingText

\paragraph{Sensitivity.}  The complete fit is repeated at the 97.5th, 99th, and 99.5th Control percentiles for ten fixed seeds (Table~\ref{tab:anomaly-sensitivity}) and with each input left out (Table~\ref{tab:anomaly-feature-ablation}).  \AnomalyFeatureAblationFindingText\  These quantities describe an operational screen; they are not measures of classification accuracy, because group membership is not a ground-truth label.

\paragraph{Group mixing.}  Weighted assortativity compares the observed same-group share of citation weight with the share expected from the row and column totals \parencite{Newman2002}:
\begin{equation}
  r=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(e)-a^\mathsf{T}b}{1-a^\mathsf{T}b},
\end{equation}
where $e$ is the $2\times2$ table of weight shares, $a$ its row totals, and $b$ its column totals.  The label-swap test swaps the two labels within each matched pair in 10,000 runs while keeping the links fixed.

\section{Reproducibility Checks}
\label{app:checks}

The rebuild checks the following invariants:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $(\mathrm{ORCID},s,\mathrm{group})$ is unique in the feature output;
  \item pairs have complete subject-keyed $h_5$ records, satisfy the three-point caliper, and do not reuse an author--subject;
  \item every edge has non-null endpoints and is unique on $(s,i,j,t)$;
  \item each per-measure complete-pair count is at most \MatchedPairCount;
  \item every observed surge share lies in $[0,1]$;
  \item one final flag supplies every flag-dependent table and figure; and
  \item reported numbers enter the manuscript through generated LaTeX files.
\end{itemize}
SQL fixtures test fractional weights, null endpoints, work-reference deduplication, subject retention, and prior-coauthor timing.  Python tests cover cohort construction, matching order, measure formulas, paired inference, clique membership and inference, the collaboration check, the screen, mixing, and flag reuse.  The workflow is offline and is run with \texttt{make reproduce}.
